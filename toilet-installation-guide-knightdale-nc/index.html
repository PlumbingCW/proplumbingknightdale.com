Basic Toilet Installation in Knightdale, NC: A Step-by-Step Guide

.

Installing a toilet takes about two hours for a first-timer. You measure the rough-in, shut off the water, remove the old toilet, check the flange, set a new wax ring and the new toilet, tighten the bolts and reconnect the supply. The two things that go wrong are buying the wrong size and ignoring a damaged flange.

This guide walks through the steps in order and tells you when to call a plumber instead.

Measure before you buy

The rough-in is the distance from the finished wall behind the toilet to the center of the floor drain. The most common size is twelve inches, but ten and fourteen inches also exist. A toilet built for twelve will not fit a ten-inch rough-in, so measure first.

Also check the space around the toilet, the shape of the bowl, which is round or elongated, and the height. An elongated bowl needs more room in front. Take a photo of the old toilet and its supply connection before you shop.

Tools and materials

Collect these before you start.

Step by step

Work slowly. Porcelain cracks if you overtighten.

  • Close the supply valve, flush and sponge out the tank and bowl.
  • Disconnect the supply line and remove the tank bolts if the tank separates.
  • Pry off the caps, unscrew the nuts on the base bolts and lift the toilet straight up.
  • Scrape the old wax from the flange and inspect it for cracks or a loose fit.
  • Set a new wax ring, lower the toilet straight down over the bolts and press it into place.
  • Add the washers and nuts and tighten them evenly, a little at a time, until the toilet is steady.
  • Connect the supply, open the valve, let the tank fill and test for leaks.

The flange is the make-or-break part

The flange is the ring on the floor that connects the toilet to the drain. It should be firmly screwed to the subfloor, level with or slightly above the finished floor and in good shape. A cracked, corroded or loose flange means the toilet will rock and leak.

Repair rings and extender kits exist for many problems, but a flange set too low, a rotted subfloor or a broken pipe is a job for a plumber. Our toilet repair page describes what we check.

Knightdale note

Slab, crawlspace and permits

Whether your bathroom sits over a crawlspace or a slab changes what you find under the old toilet. Over a crawlspace, a slow leak can rot the subfloor without being seen, so check the floor near the base for softness. Over a slab, a damaged flange is the main concern.

A like-for-like toilet swap typically does not need a permit, but moving a toilet or adding a bathroom can. Ask the Town of Knightdale Development Services, and know that plumbing inspections are handled through Wake County Permits and Inspections. Raleigh Water provides water and sewer service to Knightdale, and that connection stays untouched in a swap.

Fixture connections, traps and venting are covered by the North Carolina State Building Code: Plumbing Code, the state's edition of the International Plumbing Code (IPC), which is why a plumber checks the flange and the vent when moving a toilet.

Sources: Town of Knightdale Development Services, Wake County Permits and Inspections, Raleigh Water.

Choosing a toilet

A good toilet is more than a low price. Look for a well-regarded flush design, a fully glazed trapway that resists clogs and a flush system suited to your household. Water efficiency labels help compare models. Comfort height is easier for many people, and elongated bowls are more comfortable.

A toilet with a complicated tank fill or an unusual size is harder to repair later, so pick a common design with readily available parts.

When to call a plumber instead

Hire a pro if you find any of these.

  • The subfloor is soft, stained or rotted.
  • The flange is cracked, loose or set below the floor.
  • The drain is slow or the toilet has clogged repeatedly.
  • You cannot shut off the supply valve or it leaks.
  • The toilet is on an upper floor and a leak could cause damage below.

After the install

Check for leaks after an hour and again the next day. Look at the base for moisture and the supply connection for drips. Caulk the base only along the front and sides so a leak can show itself at the back.

If the toilet rocks, do not overtighten the bolts. Use plastic shims and trim them, then re-check the level.

Common mistakes that cause leaks

Overtightening the base bolts cracks the porcelain. Reusing an old wax ring leaks. Setting the toilet down and sliding it afterward breaks the seal. Skipping the flange inspection hides a problem that will show up as a stain on the ceiling below.

Each of these is avoidable. Lower the toilet straight down over the bolts, press once and tighten in small steps, alternating sides.

Wax rings and wax-free seals

A standard wax ring has been the default for decades and works well when the flange is in good shape. A wax-free rubber seal is reusable and easier to place if you need to lift the toilet again, and it can tolerate a flange set slightly low. Some plumbers prefer one, some the other.

Whichever you choose, use one seal only. Stacking rings on a low flange can work as a fix, but a flange that sits below the floor level needs a proper repair.

Toilet overflowing: stop the water first

Do this in order before the plumber arrives:

  1. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down, or turn the supply valve behind the toilet clockwise to shut it off.
  2. Do not flush again. Another flush adds more water to a full bowl.
  3. Put towels around the base and move rugs away.
  4. If the tub or sink also backs up, stop all water use. The problem may be the main line.
  5. Call (919) 944-7281. We answer day and night.

When to replace instead of repair

A toilet with a cracked bowl or tank should be replaced. So should one that clogs repeatedly despite a clear drain, or one whose parts are no longer available. Old, high-volume models often use more water per flush than current ones.

We help you choose a replacement that fits your rough-in distance, bowl shape and budget, then install it with a new seal, new supply line and a check for level and flange condition.

Why the flange matters

A toilet sits on a flange, the ring that connects it to the drain. Problems here are the usual reason a toilet rocks or leaks at the base, and they are easy to miss.

  • A cracked or corroded flange cannot hold the bolts properly.
  • A flange set too low or too high prevents a good seal.
  • Water leaking from the base can rot the subfloor over time.
  • We inspect the flange whenever we pull a toilet.

Many Knightdale homes have a slab or a crawlspace below the bathroom, and the leak path differs. A slow leak here is easy to ignore and expensive to ignore for long.

What decides the cost

Insurance note: a sudden overflow that damages floors may be covered. A slow base leak that has rotted the subfloor often is not. Photograph damage and keep receipts.

Water use and your toilet

A running toilet can waste a surprising amount of water without any visible leak. Raleigh Water bills Knightdale customers for water and sewer, so a silent leak shows up on your bill. A dye test in the tank is a free way to check. Add food coloring, wait fifteen minutes without flushing and look in the bowl.

Fixture connections, traps and vents are covered by the North Carolina State Building Code: Plumbing Code, the state's edition of the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and the flange check we do on every toilet job follows from it.

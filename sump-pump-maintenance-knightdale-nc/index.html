Sump Pump Maintenance and Repair: A Knightdale, NC Homeowner Guide

.

A sump pump collects water in a pit and pushes it away from the house. It sits unused most of the year and has to work during the heaviest storms, which is why testing it twice a year is the most important maintenance step. A pump that has never been tested may not run when you need it.

This guide covers how to test a pump, what usually fails and when to call a plumber.

Why some Knightdale homes have a sump pump

Most homes in Knightdale sit on slabs or crawlspaces rather than full basements, so sump pumps are less common than in other regions. They do appear where a lot sits low, where a finished lower level or crawlspace collects water, or where a drain needs help leaving the property.

If you have one, it was put there for a reason. Treat it as part of the house's defense against water and give it the same attention as a smoke alarm.

A five-minute test, twice a year

Do it in spring and fall, and before a forecast of heavy rain.

  • Look in the pit. It should be clear of debris, silt and gravel.
  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit slowly until the float lifts.
  • Confirm the pump turns on, empties the pit and shuts off.
  • Watch the discharge outside to make sure water leaves and flows away from the house.
  • Listen for grinding, rattling or a motor that hums without pumping.

If any step fails, do not wait for a storm. Call for service.

What usually fails

Most sump pump failures are simple and preventable.

Problem.Cause.Fix.
Pump will not start.Stuck float, tripped breaker or dead pump.Free the float, reset power, replace the pump.
Runs constantly.Float stuck on, check valve failed or high water table.Adjust float, replace check valve.
Runs but does not move water.Clogged intake, frozen or blocked discharge, or an air lock.Clear the intake and discharge.
Loud noise.Worn bearings or debris in the impeller.Clean or replace.
Water returns after the pump shuts off.Failed check valve.Replace the check valve.
Power outage during a storm.No backup power.Add a battery backup.

The discharge line matters as much as the pump

The pump moves water out of the pit, and the discharge line carries it away. If the line ends too close to the house, the water soaks back in and the pump runs in a loop. If the line freezes or clogs, the pump labors and burns out.

Extend the outlet well away from the foundation, slope it so it drains, and keep it clear of leaves and mulch. Never connect it to the sanitary sewer. That is not allowed in most places and can overload the sewer system.

Knightdale note

Where the water goes, and who to ask

Raleigh Water, which serves Knightdale, handles the sewer system and treats household drains, not stormwater. Sump discharge belongs on the ground away from the house, not in the sewer. If you are unsure where yours should go, ask the Town of Knightdale Development Services before you change it.

Plumbing inspections are handled through Wake County Permits and Inspections. A new sump pump or a change to the discharge can involve a permit, so ask first. In North Carolina, you can verify a plumbing contractor at public.nclicensing.org.

Drain and discharge arrangements are governed by the North Carolina State Building Code: Plumbing Code, the state's edition of the International Plumbing Code (IPC), which is one reason a sump pump should not be tied to the sanitary sewer.

Sources: Raleigh Water service information, Town of Knightdale Development Services, Wake County Permits and Inspections.

Backup power and alarms

The storms that fill a sump pit are often the storms that knock out power. A battery backup pump or a water-powered backup keeps the pit empty when the main pump has no electricity. A high-water alarm tells you before the pit overflows.

A backup is a good idea if your home has a finished space or a crawlspace you care about. We can size one for your pit and your pump.

When to replace instead of repair

Pumps wear out. Many last roughly seven to ten years, and a pump that has run heavily or stood in dirty water may fail sooner. If your pump is old, noisy or has needed repeated fixes, replacement is the sensible choice.

A new pump, a new check valve and a clean pit give you years of reliable service. We also look at the discharge line while we are there. Our plumbing installation page describes how we handle pumps and related work.

A yearly maintenance checklist

Keep it on your calendar.

  • Clean the pit and remove silt and debris.
  • Test the float and the pump.
  • Inspect the check valve, and replace it if it rattles or leaks.
  • Clear the discharge line and the outlet.
  • Test the battery backup and alarm.
  • Write the date and what you did on a tag near the pit.

Check valves, floats and why they matter

A check valve in the discharge line stops water from falling back into the pit when the pump shuts off. When it fails, the pump short-cycles, wears out quickly and may never keep up. A rattle or a thump after the pump stops is a sign the check valve is working or failing.

The float switch tells the pump when to start and stop. If it sticks against the side of the pit, the pump may not run at all or may run dry. Make sure it moves freely and that nothing in the pit can snag it.

Signs the pit itself needs work

A pit that fills with silt, a cracked liner, or a lid that does not seal can all cause trouble. Silt jams floats and burns out impellers, and an open pit can release moisture and odors into the crawlspace. Cleaning the pit and replacing a damaged lid is cheap compared with replacing a drowned pump.
